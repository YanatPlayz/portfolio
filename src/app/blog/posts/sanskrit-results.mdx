A while back, I set up the [coin-flip test](/blog/sanskrit-methodology#flipping-coins) for my 21 documented semantic shifts, a prediction for each written down in advance, and one question. Does each word move toward its new meaning? Let's see.

## 19 out of 21

**19 of the 21 shifts move in the attested direction.** Yay! Back to the coin: the probability of getting 19 or more heads out of 21 fair flips is about 0.00011. So it's very unlikely the model is just guessing.

## The fall of asura

My favorite result is *asura*. There's a nice history for this one.

In the early Ṛg-Veda, *asura* means "lord, mighty one." It's used for the biggest gods: Indra, Varuṇa, Agni [^1]. By the time of the Epics, *asura* means "demon," the classic enemy of the gods.

The plot twist is next door in Iran. The ancient Iranian cognate *ahura* went the opposite way and stayed divine (think *Ahura Mazdā*, the supreme god of Zoroastrianism) [^2]. Meanwhile, *deva*, from the same Indo-Iranian root as Avestan *daēva*, stayed "god" in Sanskrit while *daēva* became "demon" in Iranian [^3]. Two sister languages took the same pair of words and flipped them in opposite directions.

That gives me a built-in **control**. I ran *deva* through the exact same anchor set as *asura* (divine vs. demonic). If my method just pushes everything toward "demon," *deva* would move that way too.

It doesn't. In the Vedic period, *deva* and *asura* sit almost exactly the same distance from the divine and demonic anchors, each within 0.02 of even (0.65/0.67 and 0.64/0.65). That fits: at the start, both words just meant "powerful being." Then they split apart:

| Word | Δ toward "demon" |
|---|---|
| *asura* | **+0.275** |
| *deva* | **−0.159** |

*asura* heads toward the demons and *deva* goes back toward the divine. That matches the philology. I guess my models aren't so bad after all :)

## Watching the company change

The anchor test gives a single number directing the change, but I really wanted to understand what was actually happening under the hood. I inspected the nearest neighbors of each of the words, for my qualitative analysis and confirmation.

Below is a sample check, with some of *asura*'s closest words in each period:

| Era | Selected nearest neighbors |
|---|---|
| Vedic | *mahat* 'great' (#1), *devānām* 'of the gods' (#2), *viṣṇoḥ* 'of Viṣṇu' (#6), *sāvitram* 'of Savitṛ (sun deity)' (#8) |
| Upaniṣadic | *bhogāya* 'for worldly enjoyment' (#2), *abhimānam* 'ego, pride' (#10), *aviduṣām* 'of the ignorant' (#11), *sūkara* 'hog, pig' (#12) |
| Epic | *dānava* 'demon' (#1), *uraga* 'serpent' (#2), *piśāca* 'flesh-eating ghoul' (#4), *daitya* 'titan, enemy of the gods' (#10) |

<Caption>The numbers in parentheses are each word's rank among the 12 nearest neighbors.</Caption>

The table shows the pejoration story clearly. *asura* starts out among the gods, passes through a middle period where it hangs out with ego, indulgence, and ignorance, and ends up surrounded by demons and ghouls. The model learned this history purely from which words *asura* appeared next to, which I think is fascinating.

## Conclusion

This answers my first research question: yes, diachronic word embeddings *can* recover documented semantic shifts in Sanskrit, even with sandhi, heavy inflection, and a fraction of the data modern NLP is used to.

But 19 isn't 21. Next post, we look at the two words that got away, and what they say about the limits of this whole approach.
